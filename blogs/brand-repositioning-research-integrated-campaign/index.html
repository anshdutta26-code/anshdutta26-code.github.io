How I Approach a Brand Repositioning Project: Research to Integrated Campaign

Repositioning is not a new logo followed by a campaign. It is a strategic decision about what the brand should mean, to whom, and how that meaning is expressed consistently across the customer experience.

By Ansh DuttaAug 26, 20268 min read

Brand repositioning projects often attract attention because of the visible creative work. But the visible layer should come after the strategic work. Without research and a clear position, a refreshed identity can make the brand look newer without making it more relevant.

1. Understand the existing brand reality

I begin by looking at the current proposition, audiences, marketing activity, category competitors and how the brand is experienced today. The purpose is not to assume everything old is wrong. It is to identify what should be protected, what has become unclear and what opportunity exists.

2. Define the audience more precisely

A repositioning becomes more useful when it clarifies which audiences matter and what each of them needs from the brand. Different segments can have different motivations while still belonging under one coherent strategic idea.

In the Ontario Science Centre academic project, for example, the audience structure included families, educators and students, visitors to the Greater Toronto Area and corporate groups. The challenge was to serve those groups without creating four unrelated brands.

3. Find the strategic position

The position should be based on an intersection: what the audience values, what the organization can credibly deliver and where the competitive landscape leaves space for a distinctive idea.

AUDIENCE NEED ∩ BRAND CREDIBILITY ∩ COMPETITIVE SPACE ↓ POSITIONING

4. Translate position into messaging and identity

Once the strategic idea is clear, the visual identity and messaging system can reinforce it. I prefer to define a small number of messaging pillars, a tone of voice and clear rules for how the brand promise should appear across touchpoints.

Consistency does not mean sameness. The core message can stay stable while the expression changes for social, PR, search, outdoor media or an in-person experience.

5. Build an integrated campaign system

An integrated plan should connect channels around the same strategic idea rather than creating a separate concept for each platform. Experiential activity can generate a story; social can extend it; PR can add credibility; search can capture demand; outdoor can create visibility.

This was a major part of the Ontario Science Centre project, where experiential, social, OOH, PR and SEO/SEM recommendations were designed as one connected brand system.

6. Define what success would mean

Even when a repositioning project is academic or pre-launch, the strategy should specify how it would be evaluated. Depending on the objective, that could include awareness, consideration, engagement, branded search, attendance, qualified traffic or conversion.

What I try to avoid

  • Starting with visual design before the position is clear.
  • Using generic claims such as “innovative” without proof.
  • Creating channel ideas that do not reinforce the same brand meaning.
  • Ignoring internal or operational realities that affect whether the promise can be delivered.
  • Treating the repositioning as complete the moment the creative launches.

The takeaway

A strong repositioning project moves from evidence to strategy to expression. Research tells you what needs to change. Positioning defines the choice. Identity and messaging make that choice visible. Integrated communications then bring the strategy to market.

That sequence is more disciplined than beginning with a campaign idea—and it gives the creative work a much stronger reason to exist.
